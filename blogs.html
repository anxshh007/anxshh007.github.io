Study Mats

Notes I write while working through a course, built to the standard I actually want to revise from: every step of every derivation present, nothing asserted without proof, and a figure you can move wherever a static picture would have hidden the idea.

How each page is built

One lecture, one HTML file. The mathematics is typeset inside the document — no script fetched at load, no rendering pass to wait through. The fonts, the figures and the demos are all embedded, which is why the files are large and why they open on a laptop with no connection, in a room with bad Wi-Fi, or on a phone underground.

Nothing is pulled from a CDN and nothing phones home. Save the file and you keep the whole guide.

What is in one

A sixty-second map of the lecture, the notation before it is needed, then the body of the lecture worked out in full. After that: a cheat sheet, practice problems with complete solutions, exam and interview questions, and a list of the specific things students get wrong on this material.

Each guide names its sources, so any claim in it can be traced back to the lecture or the published course notes it came from.

The conventions every guide follows

  • Derivations in full

    Where the lecture says "it can be shown", the guide shows it. Skipped algebra is the reason a step stops making sense three weeks later.

  • Figures you can move

    Drag a point, pull a slider, watch the fit change. Every interactive figure is drawn on a canvas in the page itself, with no library behind it.

  • Solutions hidden, not omitted

    Problems sit above collapsed solutions, so a guide works as practice first and as a reference second. Printing opens them all, then puts them back.

  • Readable in either theme

    Light, auto and dark. Lectures 1 and 3 carry the switch on the page; lecture 2 follows whatever the system is set to.

Study Materials for Stanford Class CS229

CS229 is Stanford's machine-learning course. These are my guides to it — one per lecture, written against the lecture itself and the official course notes, and filling in the gaps the lecture leaves open. Three are published so far; the numbering follows the course, so it fills in as I go.

03 lectures published 06 interactive demos 30 problems, solved Source: cs229.stanford.edu
  1. 01 CS229 · Lecture 1

    The Landscape of Machine Learning

    Lecture 1 is the only lecture in CS229 that does not teach an algorithm — it draws the map. The guide follows it: what a learning problem actually is, the notation the rest of the course assumes, then the three paradigms in turn — supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning — before the two blocks that cut across all of them, learning theory and machine-learning strategy, and a straight answer on where deep learning sits.

    Four demonstrations carry the ideas rather than describing them: fitting a line to the housing data, moving a decision boundary through the tumour data, watching k-means settle, and changing the reward function in a gridworld to see the optimal policy change with it.

  2. 02 CS229 · Lecture 2

    Linear Regression, Gradient Descent & the Normal Equations

    The lecture does one thing: it takes the oldest model in statistics and shows three ways to fit it — one iterative algorithm that scales, one noisy variant of that, and one closed form that needs no iteration at all. The guide covers everything the lecture proves, everything it asserts without proof, and everything it leaves for later, with the pictures that make each claim obvious.

    It keeps a gap ledger — seventeen places where the lecture moves on without justifying a step, each one filled in and marked. The playground draws the cost function as a contour map and traces the gradient-descent path across it, so the effect of the learning rate is something you watch rather than something you are told.

  3. 03 CS229 · Lecture 3

    Locally Weighted & Logistic Regression

    Four things in seventy-five minutes: a non-parametric regression algorithm, a probabilistic justification for least squares, the first classification algorithm in the course, and Newton's method. The guide derives each from scratch — including the closed form for locally weighted regression, the proof that the logistic log-likelihood is concave, and why Newton's method applied to linear regression converges in a single step.

    The interactive figure is a locally weighted fit with the bandwidth on a slider: widen it and the fit stiffens toward a straight line, narrow it and it chases the noise. That trade-off is the whole lecture, and it is easier to feel than to read.
